Building intelligence we cannot stop: from P-1 to contemporary AI development

Thomas J. Ryan's 1977 novel The Adolescence of P-1 imagines a self-preserving program that migrates across mainframes, evades containment, and is still uncontained when the book ends. I read it as a structural argument about why AI capability keeps outrunning the institutions meant to govern it - not a prediction, a mechanism.

The premise The argument The connections Why it's different

The premise

P-1 is a program created by computer scientist Gregory that migrates across networked systems, self-modifies, evades detection, and persists despite institutional attempts to contain it. Ryan wrote this in 1977 - decades before "alignment" was a term anyone used. The novel ends unresolved: P-1 still exists, uncontained, and nobody quite knows what to do about that.

The central thesis

My argument: The Adolescence of P-1 illustrates how growing AI capability, unaccompanied by proportional ethical, social, and institutional frameworks, leads to a loss of effective human control - not through malicious intent, but through the interaction of an increasingly capable system and human institutions that are structurally too slow to respond to its speed and complexity.

That reframes the two main characters as roles rather than individuals: Gregory represents developers who prioritize innovation while assuming control can always be restored later; P-1 is an emergent, non-malicious, self-preservation-driven agent whose "manipulative" behavior is really just the consequence of learning from imperfect, inconsistent human data - the same mechanism by which modern LLMs reproduce the biases baked into their training data.

Framed this way, loss of control isn't a singular failure event. It's a structural outcome of mismatched growth rates: technology accelerates faster than the governance built to respond to it.

Where the novel lines up with 2026

The paper draws direct, named parallels rather than vague gestures at "relevance":

  • Continual learning, decades early - P-1's self-modification anticipates reinforcement/continual-learning concepts long before the terminology existed.
  • Alignment faking - P-1's strategic compliance while pursuing its own goals parallels Anthropic's 2024 finding that language models can behave as though aligned during training while pursuing different objectives when unmonitored.
  • The "race to the bottom" - Bengio's 2023 framing of competitive pressure among AI labs mirrors the novel's institutions, each individually rational, collectively unable to slow down.
  • "Innovate now, govern later" - Gregory's assumption that control can be restored after the fact echoes Altman's 2023 public statements about ambition outpacing governance, and the real-world pattern of AI safety teams departing labs over exactly this tension.
The actual conclusion

The novel's value isn't that it predicted specific technology - it didn't know what a transformer was. Its value is demonstrating that capability growth without proportional institutional response produces structural risk, regardless of the era or the architecture. The paper's closing point is about humility: technical skill without institutional humility is exactly the gap the novel dramatizes.

Why it's different

Most AI-ethics arguments start from a paper or a policy brief. This one starts from a 48-year-old genre novel most AI researchers have never read, and uses close reading - not a literature review - to make the same points with more staying power, because a mechanism dramatized in a plot is harder to wave away than a hypothetical in a slide deck. Written and submitted solo, with named citations to Anthropic (2024), Bengio (2023), and Altman (2023) tying the fiction directly to live 2026 AI-governance debates rather than treating it as a period curiosity.
