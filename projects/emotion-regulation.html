Master Team Project

Classifying coping strategies in grief journals with very few labels

A bereavement-support app had more than 28,000 unlabeled evening reflections and 100 labeled ones. We used active learning to decide which entries to label next, and built a detector for maladaptive coping. On a small test set of 50 entries it found all 11 maladaptive ones, but it also raised as many false alarms.

Problem Approach Results Limitations

The problem

The app asks people who lost someone to write journal entries and short evening reflections. We wanted to classify each entry into the five emotion-regulation strategies of Gross's process model (situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, response modulation), and separately into adaptive or maladaptive coping, which is the distinction that matters for support.

We started with 200 labeled journal entries and 100 labeled evening entries. The unlabeled pools had 7,201 and 28,153 entries. Labeling thousands of entries by hand was not realistic.

The approach

  • Model comparison. We compared 11 approaches: ChatGPT prompting, classical models on TF-IDF (decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, SVM, KNN, logistic regression), neural networks (NN, RNN, CNN with BiLSTM), BERT, and EmoBERTa.
  • Active learning. EmoBERTa picks the unlabeled entries it is least certain about, these get labeled, and the model is retrained. We ran four rounds and simulated the labeling.
  • Calibration and imbalance. Isotonic regression for the probabilities, and focal loss, class weights and resampling because maladaptive entries are the minority class.
  • Label checks. An annotator with a psychology background reviewed our labels across the active-learning rounds.

On journal entries, macro-F1 rose from 0.46 to 0.78 in the second round and ended at 0.62 after the fourth, so more rounds did not always help. The best maladaptive detector used a hybrid strategy: train first on a class-balanced sample, then fine-tune on the real, imbalanced distribution. It flagged every maladaptive entry in the test set, but half of its flags were adaptive entries, so it works as a first filter for a human to review, not as a final decision.

Limitations

The test set was small: 50 entries, 11 of them maladaptive. The active-learning loop was simulated against data we had already labeled, not run with a live annotator, which would be the next step for real use.
