Telling adaptive coping from maladaptive coping - with almost no labels

A real bereavement-support mHealth app had 28,153 unlabeled evening reflections and only 100 labeled ones. We used active learning to turn that into a model that catches maladaptive coping with perfect recall.

Problem Approach Results Why it's different

The problem

A grief-support app (1,600+ users, 6,500+ journal entries) records how people process loss - but almost none of it is labeled. We had two entry types (journal reflections, evening reflections) and needed to classify each into five emotion-regulation strategies from Gross's process model (Situation Selection, Situation Modification, Attentional Deployment, Cognitive Change, Response Modulation), and - more importantly clinically - into adaptive vs. maladaptive coping.

Seed labels: 200 journal entries, 100 evening entries. Unlabeled pool: 7,201 and 28,153 entries respectively. Labeling more by hand wasn't really an option.

The approach

We ran an 11-model bake-off before committing to anything:

  • Baseline sweep: ChatGPT prompting → classical ML (Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, KNN, Logistic Regression on TF-IDF) → deep learning (NN, RNN, CNN+BiLSTM) → transformers (BERT, then EmoBERTa fine-tuned on MELD/IEMOCAP).
  • Active learning: uncertainty sampling on top of EmoBERTa - the model flags which unlabeled entries it's least sure about, and only those get prioritized for (simulated) labeling.
  • Calibration: isotonic regression to fix overconfident probability estimates, checked via Expected Calibration Error and Brier score.
  • Imbalance handling: focal loss, class-weighted cross-entropy, and up/down-sampling, since maladaptive cases are the minority class that matters most.
  • Validation: we didn't just trust our own labels - an independent expert annotator with a psychology background checked labels across active-learning iterations.

Isotonic calibration cut Expected Calibration Error from 0.25 to 0.16, lifting accuracy and F1 to 0.74 with AUC 0.81. The final model was a hybrid strategy: pre-train on a class-balanced sample, then fine-tune on the real (imbalanced) distribution - capturing the minority class without losing touch with real-world frequencies.

Why it's different

This wasn't a Kaggle dataset with a leaderboard to chase - it was real clinical text where a false negative means missing someone who's coping badly with grief. That's why recall on maladaptive cases, not accuracy, was the metric we actually optimized for, and why we built in a human-expert validation loop instead of trusting our own annotations.

What I'd do differently

With more time, I'd push the active-learning loop into a real deployment cycle - right now it's simulated against a fully-labeled holdout, not run against a live annotator queue.
